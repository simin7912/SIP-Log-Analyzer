SIP Protocol Basics: Requests, Responses, Call-ID and CSeq

SIP (Session Initiation Protocol) is a text-based signaling protocol used to set up, modify, and tear down voice and video calls. If you've ever opened a raw SIP log and felt lost in a wall of headers, this guide covers the handful of concepts that make the rest click into place — and that the SIP Log Analyzer uses to reconstruct call flows automatically.

Requests and Responses

Every SIP exchange is a pair: a request from one party (like INVITE, REGISTER, or BYE) and one or more responses from the other side, identified by a three-digit status code — 100 Trying, 180 Ringing, 200 OK, or an error such as 486 Busy Here. A normal call setup is rarely a single request/response pair; it's a short sequence of provisional responses followed by a final one.

Call-ID: The Thread That Ties a Call Together

The Call-ID header is a unique identifier generated by the originating device for the entire dialog. Every message belonging to the same call — the INVITE, its responses, the ACK, and the eventual BYE — shares the same Call-ID. This is the single most useful field for reconstructing a call flow from a raw log, and it's the primary key the analyzer's sequence diagram groups messages by.

CSeq: Ordering Within a Dialog

Because a single Call-ID can carry multiple transactions over its lifetime (an INVITE, later a re-INVITE for hold/resume, then a BYE), SIP uses CSeq (Command Sequence) — a number plus a method name — to keep transactions ordered and to match each request to its correct response, even when messages arrive out of order over an unreliable transport like UDP.

From and To Tags: Identifying the Dialog

The combination of Call-ID plus the tag parameters on the From and To headers uniquely identifies a specific dialog. The To tag is assigned by the callee's side in its first reliable response, which is why you'll often see it missing on a 100 Trying but present from 180 Ringing onward.

Why This Matters for Debugging

When a call fails, the fastest path to a root cause is usually: find the Call-ID, pull every message with that Call-ID in order, and read the CSeq/status code sequence. That is exactly what a sequence diagram automates — instead of scrolling through thousands of interleaved log lines from multiple simultaneous calls, you see one call's lifecycle top to bottom.
